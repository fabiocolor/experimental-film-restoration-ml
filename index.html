Experimental Film Restoration with Machine Learning

Faded colour brought back, decayed nitrate healed, films beyond repair rebuilt, with open-weight models running on local hardware. The film's own picture stays the record; the model only supplies what is lost.

Where things stand Run it yourself

Independent research by Fabio Bedoya, done alongside commercial restoration work in London, into what open-weight AI models can do for the archives that can't afford the commercial route, and how far those models can be trusted. If you think about it, digital restoration was never really about recovery. The usual tools copy information from the frames around the damage and mask it. These models can put back what was actually lost, and that is new. It is also where the risk is, so the rule is the same in every area here: start from the scan, keep its picture, use a model only for what is missing, and write down what the model did. Nothing is automatic. Every result is reviewed at full size before it goes up, and every example has a record of its source, models and settings.

Six research areas

  1. Faded colour recovery

    When the faded scan is all that survives. The colour is rebuilt from the film itself and carried through whole shots, pans, crowds, dancing and all, while every frame keeps the scan's own brightness, grain and resolution.

    How it works, five shots and two kits

  2. Temporal CbCr

    When something with the colour still survives: a DVD, a telecine, an overlapping negative, or a set of reviewed frames. A small model learns the colour of that one shot in about 90 seconds and carries it through every frame.

    How it works, examples and the Nuke template

  3. Diffusion healing

    Nitrate decay, a gap of fifteen frames, emulsion breaking up. Far beyond what Phoenix or DIAMANT can interpolate. Everything that survives is used first, a video model generates only the missing picture, and every generated pixel is on record.

    How it works, examples and kit

  4. Diffusion reconstruction

    For films where every frame is damaged and normal tools have nothing clean to borrow from. A video model rebuilds the picture from what survives, shot after shot, to one look for the whole reel. The result is called what it is: a reconstruction, with a synthetic quality the film never had.

    How it works, a whole reel and kit

  5. Black-and-white colourization

    Colour for films shot in black and white, fitted to the stock that recorded them, so that the greys on the film are exactly what that stock would have seen of the colour. Whole shots, full resolution, the picture untouched. The palettes are an interpretation, and every page says so.

    How it works, and public-domain examples

  6. Diffusion Upscaling

    A separate research idea for reference-based video resolution/detail enhancement. No workflow or result has been qualified for this new project.

    Scope and evidence state

Run it yourself

Not everyone has a room full of GPUs, so the two single-frame colour graphs run in Comfy Cloud with no card and no installation. The whole-shot, healing and reconstruction kits need a local NVIDIA card. Every graph is published laid out with notes, so you can read it node by node and see what each step is supposed to do, and every kit is checked against the published result and says how close it gets.
